Making the feedback library more engaging

Three options, isolated for review before any of them touch the real build. All three replace decoration with ownership: the colour on a survey comes from the department that owns it, so the grid gains colour and gains meaning at the same time.

Where the colour comes from

Nothing here is invented. Elemental background tints are the only permitted coloured backgrounds in the system, and only for the element that already owns the content — so the question was never "which colour looks nice", it was "who owns this survey". Two surveys answer it by name: the sub-brand table fixes CollabSales to Gold and CollabMedia to Water. The rest follow their department.

  • Gold · Amber CollabSales · Finance · Legal · QA & Audit
  • Water · Navy CollabMedia · Data · Logistics · Exec Strategy
  • Earth · Green HR · People Ops · Customer Success
  • Wood · Salmon Product Design · Strategy · R&D
  • Fire · Orange Marketing · PR · Brand — no survey owns it yet
  • Data changeOne owner field per entry in FORMS — name, element, glyph
  • ReusesThe sub-brand element table and the department table, unchanged
  • Why it mattersRotating tints by grid position is explicitly forbidden — "don't tint a card with an element that doesn't own its content"

Baseline — what the grid looks like today

For comparison. Every signal on the tile is monochrome except the status badge, so the eye has nothing to sort by until it reads.

1Icon chip + subject glyph

The chip carries the owner's colour, the glyph says what the survey is about, and the owner's name sits under the title as caption text — colour reinforces, words state. The chip rides the badge row rather than claiming one of its own, so it costs only the 16px by which a 36px chip out-measures a 20px badge; the owner line accounts for the rest, and the tile lands 34px taller than the baseline above. This is the variant the spec itself prefers: "the standard way a card declares which department owns it."

  • ReusesThe shared .icon-chip already in components.css, and Iconography's Fill weight for card headers
  • NewFive element modifiers — 10 lines of CSS, one promotable candidate
  • RiskNone to the layout. Chip tint is 12%, not the 8% card token — the two are not interchangeable

2Response count as a shape

The same tiles, with the footer's bare number turned into a 4px rail filled in the owner's colour. Progress bar already permits a department-colour fill override, and tone-gold / tone-water / tone-earth already exist in components.css — this option needs no new CSS at all. It also makes the grid scannable: a half-empty rail is a survey that needs chasing.

This option needs a decision, not just a build. A rail needs a response target, and surveys do not have one today. Onboarding check-in (third tile) shows the honest fallback: no target, no rail — it runs continuously, so there is nothing to be a fraction of. If most surveys end up targetless, this option is doing less work than it looks.

  • ReusesProgress bar as specified — size-compact plus the existing tone-* element fills
  • NewNo CSS. One target field, and a footer that can stack
  • RiskNeeds a target per survey; the over-target case (204 of 200) has to clamp the fill but keep the true number

3Element-tinted card — detail screen only

Full 8% tint on the surface. On a detail screen this is clean: one survey, one owner, one tint, which is exactly the condition the rule asks for. The nested panel steps up to the 16% -strong token rather than going white — that step is what the -strong tokens exist for. Hover the card: Gold's own motion entry is "brief glint on hover", so the sweep is spec, not garnish.

Recommendation

Ship 1, hold 2, take 3 on the detail screen only. Option 1 gets most of the visual lift for ten lines of CSS and one data field, and it breaks no rule. Option 2 is free to build but needs a product decision about targets first. Option 3 is genuinely good in one place and genuinely bad in another, and the two demos above are there so that call can be made by looking rather than by arguing.

One thing none of these three touch: the bug reports. Severity owns colour on that surface, and an elemental tint there would be read as a severity signal. Worth leaving alone.
